# Give Dalecrest residents something worth following

**Design background · 21 November 2026 · code reviewed at `3a49911`.** This was written as an independent proposal. Its central idea, the Mabel–Oswin promise, is now the first milestone of the [canonical roadmap](roadmap.md), and its division of responsibilities is the accepted [resident knowledge graph](knowledge-graph.md). It is kept for its reasoning and its checks. Its stage list and its closing comparison with the earlier roadmap are superseded. Where this document and the roadmap differ, the roadmap governs.

**Build one promise that can survive a night's sleep.** Start with Mabel Ash, whose oven needs wood, and Oswin Holt, who wants to keep it supplied. Can Mabel ask for help, can Oswin turn her down or remember his word tomorrow, and can someone who missed their meeting find out what happened? That small story tests the intelligence, the economy, both village views, and the reason to visit again. It also has a perfectly good quiet ending if the two never come to an agreement.

## Who sets a goal and who acts on it

The division of responsibilities below is now the accepted baseline. The initial choices are the project's general-purpose Claude model for occasional goal setting and the existing Decisions API for choosing what to do next. Today Dalecrest supplies predefined goals to the action model. [Resident knowledge graph](knowledge-graph.md) · [Current request](../decisions.mjs)

| When | Who decides | What persists |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A resident needs a new direction, or learns something that changes an existing goal | A general-purpose model reviews personality, needs, known facts and unresolved commitments. It can keep, revise, finish or abandon a small set of personal goals. The engine checks referenced people and facts. | A versioned goal with its supporting memories and a recognizable completion condition. A promise to someone else keeps its own status even if it loses priority. |
| A resident wakes, finishes a task or encounters a meaningful interruption | The action model sees the active goals, current needs, relevant observations and actions the resident can start. It chooses one, with room for hunger, rest or friendship to take precedence. | The selected action, the goal versions in context, and the exact request and reply. |
| The chosen task is underway | The engine handles the route, elapsed time, stock and physical consequences. If the task becomes impossible, the next model choice uses the updated situation. | The actual result linked to that choice. Progress toward a goal follows from observed outcomes. |

For example, suppose Alice heard at supper that water ran short. Her goal-setting model might choose “Have four buckets in the common barrels by dusk.” When she wakes hungry, the action model can choose breakfast first. The goal survives that meal. If she later chooses to draw water, one choice covers walking to the well and filling the buckets. If someone else fills the barrels first, the village records who did it and Alice can learn that her goal has been met.

Start with existing goals and add reflection for a small pilot. Reuse a goal across turns, combine changes learned close together, and give goal reviews their own spending cap so residents still get action turns. An unavailable goal model leaves the last valid goal in place. Store the exact goal-setting exchange in PostgreSQL and link its goal version to later decision traces. Compare action-only and two-model runs on the same starting situations and total spending allowance. Measure real wait and cost per completed task, and inspect whether residents remember obligations and adapt when the world changes.

## A first story to build

The following is a proposed acceptance scenario. The starting conditions can be set in a test village. The outcome stays open in live play.

1. **Mabel learns something.** At the oven she observes that there is flour and no wood. The village records when and where she learned it. Oswin at the coppice has his own observations.
2. **Someone makes a choice.** When the two meet, Mabel can ask Oswin to stock the common store by dusk tomorrow. She can also gather wood herself or spend her time elsewhere. If she asks, Oswin hears the request. At his next turn he can accept, decline or leave it unanswered. He may already be tired or owe someone else his time.
3. **The choice survives ordinary life.** If Oswin accepts and then sleeps, the promise, its deadline and its beneficiary are still in his next relevant decision. He can choose the existing woodcutting job to fulfill it. Dalecrest credits the promise when his shift actually adds wood. Another worker supplying the store can relieve the shortage without earning Oswin credit.
4. **Both the audience and the residents get an ending.** Mabel may subsequently choose to bake. If flour has run out, the oven stays cold. If the deadline passes with the promise open, Oswin knows he missed it. Mabel can learn what happened by checking the store or speaking to him. A viewer can follow the request, the reply, the work and its consequence, including “still unanswered.”

That first version needs one resource, one kind of promise, and the existing communal inventory. Personal inventories, delivery routes and an economy with prices can wait until a second story requires them.

Stages 1 and 2 are the first milestone. A frontend contributor can prototype Stage 3 against fixed outcome examples while the backend builds it. Connect that prototype once the same examples pass through PostgreSQL. Use the viewer tests as proposed acceptance targets. The return-visit pilot supplies the first audience baseline.

## Implementation choices that matter

**Remember what is consequential.** Keep the resident's existing JSONB record for identity, needs and current activity. Give a two-party commitment one durable record with both actor IDs, terms, due village minute, status and originating event ID. Store each resident's knowledge with a source and observation time. At decision time include open obligations, the last known fact needed for a feasible option, and a pertinent encounter with the other party. Bound their size. A public oven tally or an announced moot can become common knowledge by an explicit rule. A visitor looking for someone can act on a last-known location and find they have moved. [Current state design](design.md)

Apply the same rule to the action menu. Removing stock counts from the prompt buys little if “Bake” disappears the instant a distant resident uses the last wood. Away from the oven, Mabel can always choose “Go check the oven.” Once there, the engine can offer jobs she can actually start. The trip itself has an observable outcome even if there is no fuel.

**Distinguish a reason the viewer can verify from a motive the system would have to guess.** The request proves Oswin knew about a promise. The response proves which action he selected and reports model confidence. The stock change proves whether wood arrived. A choice like “Cut wood for Mabel's request” can explicitly reference the commitment. A plain “Cut wood” establishes only the work and its result. Give the chronicle that distinction. Keep raw requests, responses and confidence in the backend trace. Use factual templates for the first recaps. If a cheaper general-purpose model later writes a daily digest, give it a separate budget and require a supporting event for each claim. [Decision trace](decision-tracing.md)

**Keep decisions affordable enough to see them play out.** The default village budget is 7,000 tokens per real hour. At an illustrative 400 tokens per request that buys roughly 17 turns across the whole village, before retries. An extra request per greeting competes with somebody waking up or finishing a shift. Start by measuring actual token charges per applied action, median and 95th-percentile real wait, longest wait by resident, and completed commitments per village day. Capture when an actor enters the queue in wall-clock time, including budget waits before the API request. Compare the same population and budget before adding residents. Keep travel and routine gestures mechanical. Reserve Luna for choices, including social replies that create obligations. A service wait remains visible as a wait. [Budget and queue](../action-turns.mjs) · [Clock behavior](design.md)

**Make scenes readable at village scale.** Give Mabel a peel at the oven and Oswin a working pose at the woodpile. Change the stack when the stock changes and light the oven during an active bake. On the map, highlight the selected resident's destination and the other participant when visible. In the walking view, offer a camera framing both speakers and keep manual control available. Give the same facts text labels for night scenes, small screens and reduced motion. The first visual test is whether a viewer can distinguish waiting, working and a completed batch with the sound off. [Map](../public/map.js) · [Walking view](../public/view3d.js)

**Give a returning viewer a reliable place to resume.** Query history by participant or commitment, page by event ID and save the last seen ID per follow. An open request can stay visible for days with no fresh event. Show that status and the last update. Link to a dated chronicle entry and offer a separate jump to the person's current location. Existing events can still appear as dated text. Add explicit links for new events as they occur, and avoid assigning causes retroactively from similar wording. [Snapshot query](../db.mjs) · [HTTP routes](../server.mjs)

## What I would put into the combined plan

Fund the two-resident slice and one viewer test first. Use recorded or scripted choices to test every branch cheaply, then use budgeted real Luna turns to learn whether observations and obligations influence behavior. Track both results separately. If richer prompts consume more turns than the improvement warrants, shorten the observations and test again before increasing the hourly budget.

The decisions for synthesis are how long a promise should last in **real viewing time**, which facts the audience may see before the residents learn them, and what audience influence belongs in the original village after a separate pilot. My starting assumptions are a visitor who watches for two minutes and checks back tomorrow, a public record of observable events, and residents who retain the final say over their own actions.

## After reading the other proposal

The other review changes my ordering in two places. First, measure the time a visitor waits to see someone act, alongside the test of Alice's memory. The roadmap observed a 49-second wait for the next village minute in one live snapshot. Its longer-run activity counts came from the local simulation. Together they identify a pacing question that deserves a production-path test. Second, bring resident links, event markers and a chronicle that highlights consequential changes into the promise pilot. Then someone can send a friend directly to Mabel's predicament and both can check back. [Other agent's roadmap](roadmap.md)

The main design disagreement is who decides what Oswin does after waking. In the roadmap's Stage 0, a model supplies daily priorities and local scores choose his activity. I would borrow its longer planning horizon and let a model choose the next executable task, such as “stock the oven for Mabel.” Walking to the coppice, cutting and stocking can be phases of that one choice. Completion or changed circumstances return control to the action model. Compare spending per completed task and visible wait against today's single-activity turns before committing to a clock speed. [Current decision design](design.md) · [Roadmap Stage 0](roadmap.md)

I would borrow the roadmap's days-of-food measure and its distinction between warmth and trust for a second story. Doubling granary capacity with the same grain should leave the estimated days of food unchanged. Mabel can remain fond of Oswin while becoming less willing to rely on him after a missed promise. Both changes give a later choice a concrete reason to differ.
