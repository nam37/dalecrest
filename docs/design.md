# Dalecrest design decisions

These decisions describe the running village. When a change alters a decision, record the reason here and update any affected operator instructions. Planned changes are in the [roadmap](roadmap.md) and are not described here until they are built.

With each visitor-facing release, add a short entry to the [public development updates](../public/development.html). Keep entries newest first, group changes published on the same Eastern date, and describe what visitors can see or do.

### 1. The village clock belongs to the server

Villagers should be able to finish a shift or meet a friend while nobody is watching. The Node.js server advances the simulation in village minutes. It locks the world row before each tick and commits the new world, resident states, events, and active decision traces together. A browser reads a snapshot. Pausing the view freezes only that observer’s snapshot. When the server stops, the clock stops at its last committed tick.

### 2. Keep a current record for each resident and a shared village history

PostgreSQL stores each resident in `dalecrest.actors`, keyed by a stable ID. The `state` JSONB document holds their identity, desires, needs, position, activity, relationships, memories, and current plan. JSONB lets a resident’s record grow without requiring a new table for every trait. The shared clock and supplies live in `dalecrest.world`. Events with a village minute, participants, description and a salience go into `dalecrest.events`. The public chronicle shows the latest 100 notable events, and the full record, routine included, remains queryable in PostgreSQL and from the Chronicle page.

```sql
SELECT id, state->>'name' AS name, state->'desires' AS desires,
       state->'action' AS activity, state->'plan' AS plan
FROM dalecrest.actors
ORDER BY ordinal;
```

### 3. Luna chooses at each real choice, and the clock does not wait

With the OpenAI provider enabled, the Decisions API chooses what a resident does whenever there is something to decide. The request includes feasible activities, the resident's goals and needs, recent memories, and the last flour and firewood count they personally made at an oven. That count includes its age. A resident can choose a reachable oven inspection without knowing what it will reveal. Baking requires a prior check at that oven, physical presence and a remembered supply sufficient for a batch. If those supplies have since run out, the baker discovers the obstacle on starting the shift and counts again. Other activities still use the existing site, capacity and availability rules. Luna selects one menu ID. Under the village clock's database lock, Dalecrest rechecks the offered action and records its start. The simulation resolves movement, encounters and subsequent outcomes. [Trace an oven inspection](decision-tracing.md#trace-an-oven-inspection)

**Routine follows a standing instruction.** Measured under local rules, a resident reaches about 23 activity boundaries a village day, 55% of them repeat the activity before, and one night's sleep is five of them. Asking the model at every boundary spent the budget on moments where nothing had changed. A fresh choice is now kept as a standing instruction for the routine it answered: a night's sleep, a meal, or another stretch of the same activity. Staying on with the same friend in the same place is another stretch of a visit. Visiting anyone else, or following the friend somewhere new, is a fresh choice. At a boundary the engine follows a valid instruction. It asks for a fresh decision when no instruction covers the moment, when the instruction has lapsed or the resident's goals have changed, when the resident has heard something new, when two needs are urgent at once, when the instructed action cannot start, or when following it would run into something the resident has a stake in. A moot or an accepted promise can bring about that choice. A stale instruction is dropped with its reason and never followed. The engine never picks an activity itself. The rules and their starting values are in `engine/standing.mjs`. They are pilot parameters.

**The clock advances on its own schedule.** Earlier, ticks slowed to fit the token budget and stopped when everyone was waiting. The clock now runs independently of the model queue. In fixed mode, `VILLAGE_SECONDS_PER_MINUTE` sets the rate, with 30 real seconds per village minute by default with OpenAI and 1 under local rules. `VILLAGE_CLOCK_MODE=day-night` runs every village minute in order at 15 seconds from 06:00 to 22:00 and 7.5 seconds overnight. A committed minute determines the duration of the interval that follows it. Map and Walk read that duration from the same snapshot. After a long interruption the clock resumes the next minute from where it stopped. A resident whose choice has not arrived waits where they are. Their needs keep rising and everyone else carries on. If a valid instruction covers the moment, they follow it, and the later reply is marked superseded.

The backend checks for waiting residents every two seconds. A choice that something is about to overtake is served first, then whoever has waited longest. Failures have a one-minute per-turn cooldown. Residents receive 48,000 tokens per rolling real hour, with a 60,000 allowance for Robin’s reporting rounds. All share the village ceiling of 480,000. A resident who has used their allowance yields to the next eligible resident. Set `OPENAI_ACTION_TOKENS_PER_HOUR` to lower the village limit. The hosted stack sets `VILLAGE_MIN_RESIDENTS` to ten. The clock welcomes newcomers using available hall beds in the same transaction as the population and arrival records. The backend reserves an estimate before sending, then replaces it with the reported usage. A quarter-hour of spending can be used in a burst and replenishes continuously. Other provider modes retain the local activity scoring system.

**Every activity records how it began.** `dalecrest.activity_log` holds one row for each fresh choice, each activity started on a standing instruction, each wait and each instruction that ended, with the exchange behind it. The world row counts ticks and the latest any tick has run. `node --env-file=.env tools/pilot-report.mjs` reports the pilot measurements from those records. [Roadmap Stage 0](roadmap.md) · [Reading the activity log](decision-tracing.md)

### 4. Preserve the exchange and observe the outcome separately

Residents can tell neighbours about an oven reading or a completed shift. A low count can also give them a reason to ask a neighbour to cut wood, grind flour, gather barley or draw water. Counts of flour and wood come from oven inspections. Someone depositing barley or water sees the balance of that reserve. A report keeps the original witness and observation time through retellings. Walking to speak to someone can end in a missed visit. The recipient learns the news when the two meet within speaking distance.

Residents can approach neighbours who are awake and have arrived in the village. A conversation that requires travel is offered as an attempt to find the person. If the two miss each other, the speaker recalls the failed visit in their next decision. For 30 village minutes, another visit to that neighbour stays off the menu unless the two come within speaking distance and the neighbour has stopped travelling. Choices involving other neighbours and private decisions about requests remain available.

Each request asks for one shift's output within 24 village hours. The model can select an agreement, a refusal, an ordinary activity or, after agreeing, the shift naming the request. Only verified production on that named shift earns credit. Accepting, declining, reporting and fulfilling a request always require fresh choices. The current date and deadlines in model requests use the same calendar as the observatory. [Trace a request and its reply](decision-tracing.md#trace-a-request-and-its-reply)

Before a helper repeats a routine, the engine estimates when they could finish the promised shift afterward, including both walks. A routine that would leave less than the pilot's 30-minute margin triggers a fresh decision. When the shift is unavailable, the deadline itself still interrupts a routine that would run into it. The helper sees how many village minutes remain and can still choose to rest.

When a requester learns a newer fact about that supply, their next free turn asks whether to keep the request open, withdraw it in person or decide later. A store count records the balance at a particular time. A work report records how much was added. Either can change the resident's assessment. Keeping or deferring is private. A withdrawal reaches the helper only when they meet. The rule applies to firewood, flour, barley and water. [Trace a reconsideration](decision-tracing.md#trace-a-reconsideration)

An actor’s current plan is overwritten by later plans. A chronicle entry alone cannot recover the exact prompt or the model’s confidence. Each OpenAI attempt therefore gets a durable trace ID before the HTTP call. The trace links the exact request and reply to the simulation’s subsequent activities and chronicle entries. Each direct action records its start and subsequent outcomes under the same trace ID. The trace is available through PostgreSQL and excluded from the public village snapshot. [Trace schema and queries](decision-tracing.md).

### The morning paper

Robin Marsh makes reporting rounds at public places. His eyewitness notes are permanent village events. At 06:00 the clock queues a dated Dalecrest Daily covering the previous midnight-to-midnight village day. The Claude editor reads the complete day’s public records and Robin’s notes, plus the previous edition and its cited evidence for follow-ups. Private request reviews, planner decisions and the archive’s own housekeeping records stay out of the editor’s material. The editor writes as Robin, who enjoys cataloguing the day and may give his opinion of it, but may state as fact only what a record says. It is told when a thing happened as a part of the day, not a clock time. Each published paragraph links to its supporting records. A unique edition per day, a renewable-on-retry claim and ordered publication let the press resume after an interruption. [Reading the Daily and configuring the editor](../README.md#read-the-dalecrest-daily)

### 5. Deploy the application independently of the village data

The VPS runs the application and a dedicated PostgreSQL 17 service in separate containers. The database uses a private network and persistent volume. Caddy serves the observatory over HTTPS. A systemd timer polls GitHub `main` about every two minutes. The deployer builds and tests the candidate, takes a database dump, switches the application container, and checks that it can read the residents. A failed health check can roll back the app image. Restoring village data is a separate operator decision. Administered host files and their installation paths are described in [the deployment scripts](../deploy/).

### 6. Choose the model by what the simulation needs back

The accepted design assigns personal goals to a general-purpose model, the choice of the next feasible action to the Decisions API, and the actual outcome to the simulation engine. A goal persists across activities until the resident has reason to reconsider it. The resident knowledge graph describes the evidence each role receives and the links PostgreSQL should preserve. [Resident knowledge graph](knowledge-graph.md)

Today `gpt-6-luna` makes fresh choices in OpenAI mode. A still-valid instruction can repeat a previous choice. Since 22 November 2026 Claude Haiku 5.5 (`claude-haiku-5-5`) reviews residents' goals through Anthropic’s Messages API, using the backend environment variable `CLAUDE_KEY`, as decision 14 describes. [Claude models and pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/models/overview) · [Messages API](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/api/messages/create)

### 7. The ground is generated once and then owned by the village

The first map was a drawing: coordinates typed into the page, with a second copy of the eight sites in the simulation. Nothing could be built because nothing knew where anything was. The map is now one JSON document in `dalecrest.map`, generated from a seed by `engine/worldgen.mjs`. Roads come first: a through road, lanes out to the mill, fields and coppice, then buildings on lots along those ways, fields and woods. The document holds the road graph, every place with its footprint and door, the trees and the terrain parameters. Elevation and water are pure functions of those parameters, so no height grid is stored.

The seed is used once. After founding, the stored document is the truth and the generator is never run again for that village, so improving the generator cannot reshuffle a village people are watching. `VILLAGE_SEED` chooses the seed for a new database. A database from before the map existed is given one at startup: residents keep their records, each household receives a cottage, and legacy site names are mapped to places.

During that first migration, residents working or waiting move to the corresponding place on the new map. Residents already travelling start from their new home and continue toward their chosen destination. A rollback to a release predating the generated map requires a compatible village database. Restore the matching pre-release dump into a separate database and verify it before switching the app.

Residents walk the road graph. A route is stored with the action as a list of waypoints, which keeps the simulation resumable from JSON alone and lets the page animate walking along the lanes between snapshots. Where open ground is clearly shorter, residents cut across it. Twelve crossings between the same two places wear a path into the map.

The map changes rarely and is larger than a resident record, so it has its own table and revision. The world row carries the current revision. The server holds one copy per process and reloads it when the revisions differ. The page fetches `/api/map` only when the revision in the snapshot changes.

### 8. The village grows from what residents ask for

A lodger can propose a cottage while a grower asks for a field. Neighbours can witness the proposal or hear about it later. Each telling retains the observation it came from, even when a secondhand estimate changes. A resident who knows about a motion can speak for it, oppose it or reserve judgment. Enough backers put it on the moot’s agenda.

Attendance at the hall opens a ballot on each motion. In the model-driven village each voter chooses through the Decisions API with their own goals, obligations and observations. The hall reading establishes the official terms and preserves any earlier report the voter heard. A quorum is at least three residents and at least 40% of the village. More than half of attendees must support a motion to pass it. An opposing majority rejects it. A divided vote, missing ballots or insufficient quorum postpones it. The record distinguishes an explicit abstention from an unreturned ballot.

An agreed work needs a site, a crew and the labour and materials in its catalogue entry. Workers draw wood as they build. The moot can also raise the daily adult bread allowance from 1.25 to 1.5 loaves, effective at the next issue. The Chronicle can attach a labelled model-written reconstruction to the recorded result.

The loop that drives it: a village with bread on the table, land to feed one more and room at the hall draws a traveller off the road. They take up the trade the village is shortest of and lodge at the hall, which starts the case for a cottage. More residents strain the fields, bins, well and oven in turn. When the lots near the green are taken, the residents stake out a new lane. Arrivals slow as the village grows and stop at 36 residents.

The sandbox records its rule-based votes with their source. The hosted village links each returned ballot to the voter’s exact model exchange. The ten latest moots stay in village state and the full roll calls remain in PostgreSQL. [Follow a ballot](decision-tracing.md#follow-a-ballot)

### 9. Two views draw the same stored map

The plan view in `public/map.js` draws the map in ink on parchment, in the manner of Watabou's village generator. The walking view in `public/view3d.js` raises the same footprints into low-poly buildings with three.js and follows the selected resident at eye level. Neither view holds layout of its own, so anything the village builds appears in both. A site shows as stakes, then a frame, then the finished building.

three.js is an npm dependency served from `/vendor/`, so the page loads nothing from another host. The typefaces (IM Fell English and Alegreya, from the `@fontsource` packages) are served the same way.

Both views follow the village clock. The map takes a night wash with lit windows, and the walking view has a sky, sun, moon and stars. A hearth with someone at it smokes in both. A resident at home is indoors and not drawn: their window and chimney stand in for them. `public/ambient.js` holds the rules the two views share. The engine modules are served to the browser unchanged. The Sandbox at `/atlas.html` uses them to found a village from any seed in the page and run it forward by days, which is the quickest way to judge the look of a grown village without waiting for one or touching a database.

In the live observatory, Map and Walk follow the same selected resident and published clock. A waiting resident shows their finished activity and the village time they entered the decision queue. Movement follows recorded positions and the time until the next tick. Pausing holds both the displayed village and its map revision. The Sandbox uses browser-local rules and spends no model tokens.

A chronicle entry carries the place where it happened. The map pins a note there for an hour and a half, and the walking view plants a staff with a pennant and a note. **Show where** beside an entry turns whichever view is open to that spot.

A visitor who arrives with no resident in the address is shown around by the director in `public/director.js`. It reads the same snapshot the page draws and ranks what is worth watching by fixed rules: a resident keeping a promise or speaking with a neighbour first, then the moot, building, a resident deciding, trades, meals and leisure, with anyone indoors last. It stays with a scene for about thirteen seconds and goes round the others before returning. A chronicle entry less than three village minutes old cuts in once the scene on show has had five seconds, and is told once. When everyone is indoors it shows the whole parish. The caption names what is in view and repeats the recorded basis of the activity, in the words the resident card uses. The director has no model and adds no claim of its own about motive. Choosing a resident, opening a link to one, or moving the map ends the watch. The Watch button starts it again, and turning it off there is remembered in the browser.

### 10. The village keeps a calendar

A count of days ("Day 3") told a visitor how long the server had been running, not when the village is. The clock is still a count of village minutes, and `engine/calendar.mjs` turns it into a date. Dalecrest was founded on Tuesday, 1 August 1312, the start of harvest, and each village day is the next day of the Julian calendar that England kept, with its real weekdays and leap years. The founding date is one constant. Nothing is stored, so an existing village simply gains its dates. The page shows the date on the clock, in chronicle headings and wherever a past moment is mentioned. The seasons follow this calendar.

### 11. The year has weather, and winter burns the woodpile

Under local rules the wood store never fell below 10 of 32 in sixty-day runs, so the oven could not run short and a promise about firewood had nothing to be about. The roadmap asked for one ordinary pressure. Winter fuel is that pressure.

**The sky is computed, not stored.** `engine/seasons.mjs` turns a village minute and the map's seed into the season, the hours of daylight, and the weather: temperature, cloud, rain, snow and snow lying. Like the calendar it is a pure function, so a stored village replays under the same sky and an existing village simply gains its weather. Weather holds for three hours at a time. Days are 7½ hours long in mid-December and 16½ in June, at an English latitude, with the Julian calendar's eight-day lag behind the sun.

**Each hearth burns its own household's wood.** At dawn every hearth burns its day's firewood: nothing on a mild day, half a bundle on an ordinary winter's day and a whole one in hard frost. Since 22 November 2026 a cottage burns from its own woodpile, which its people fill by carrying firewood home from the common store when they choose to. The hall, while anyone lodges there, still draws on the common store. A household whose pile runs out wakes to a cold house and remembers why, though the village woodpile may stand full. It is their own observation, in the sense of the [resident knowledge graph](knowledge-graph.md): nobody else is told. Before households kept their own piles, every hearth drew on the common store and some went cold in turn when it ran short.

**Cold is a fourth need.** It rises out of doors in cold air and in a house without a fire, and falls beside one. A chilled resident sleeps badly. The action model receives it with the other needs, along with the weather in a few words and, for those it happened to, the cold hearth.

**Two rules make winter bite.** Without them the woodwards simply cut more, because they were idle most of the day. The fields and the coppice are worked by daylight only, from first light until half an hour before sunset. And snow makes poor woodcutting: a shift brings home two bundles in falling or thin snow and one when it lies deep, against three in open weather.

**What it produced.** Unscripted, from founding to early April under local rules and the common-store hearths: seed 26113 had 6 days with cold hearths and 15 mornings with flour and no firewood, seed 26112 had 1 and 7, and seed 26111 had none and 1. Nobody starved. With household woodpiles, seed 26113 had 37 mornings on which one household woke cold while the village woodpile stood. `seasons.test.mjs` holds the mornings with flour and no firewood as a test.

**Both views show it.** The map repaints once a village day: barley goes from plough to green to gold to stubble, broadleaved trees leaf, turn and go bare, and lying snow whitens ground, fields and roofs. Rain and snow fall across the plate, cloud greys the light, and a house with no fire shows no smoke and no lit window. The walking view does the same with its sky, fog, falling rain and snow, and the colours of land, trees and roofs. There a cold house loses its smoke and the glow round its window, though the pane itself still lights with the rest at night. The clock gives the season and the weather.

**Not done.** Crops do not follow the season yet, so barley is still gathered in January. Sleep keeps fixed hours all year. The wood store holds 32 bundles whatever the size of the village, which is a little over a day's burning for thirty hearths in frost. `node --env-file=.env tools/winter-village.mjs` runs a preview village that wakes on 1 January, to look at all this without waiting four village months.

### 12. The year has to be lived through

Stage 3 gave residents more to weigh, with engine rules and no model calls of its own. It was built on 22 November 2026. Two of the roadmap's open decisions were settled first: the year is the real one, and the live village does not know death.

**The harvest has a season.** `engine/stores.mjs` sets how much barley a field ripens each day by the month: nothing from November to March, a little in April, most in August. A year is good or poor for barley, fixed by the map's seed, and heavy rain at harvest lays some of the crop flat. A field holds thirty measures before the rest is lost standing. Whatever is in store when the fields stop has to last until they ripen again. The founding village has a barn for 160 measures, each granary adds 120, and barley in store is slowly lost to mice and damp. Food is counted in days: what the stores would feed everyone for. Residents ask for a granary when the barn and granaries could not see the village through a winter, and nobody worries over a granary with room to spare. Nobody comes up the road in winter, and a newcomer comes only when the stores would feed them to the next harvest. The lord's reeve takes a tenth of the barley at Michaelmas and Lady Day. Under local rules seed 26111 ended its first winter with one measure of barley in store.

**Households keep their own.** `engine/households.mjs`. Each cottage has a woodpile and a larder. Carrying firewood home and drawing the household's bread at the storehouse are choices on the menu, and a resident can eat from the larder at home. Children eat from the larder, and an empty one means a hungry morning that the parents remember.

**Lives run their course.** `engine/lives.mjs`. Residents have ages and a sex, which the founders' records and the parish's given names supply. Days of hunger, cold or overwork bring illness. A resident too ill to work is offered no work, and mends when fed, warm and rested. Two residents who are close, trust each other and have a spark between them, which only some close pairs do, walk out together. After a month either may ask the other to marry. The asking and the answer are their own choices on the menu, made by the Decisions API like any other. A wedding is held on the green on a Sunday, and the couple keep one house. A married couple can have children, who live in the household and at fourteen become residents. A lodger moves into a cottage that stands empty. A resident who is ill too long, goes hungry for days or waits two months at the hall with no cottage in prospect leaves Dalecrest, with any children. Death, by a long illness or by old age, comes only where `world.mortal` is set. The Sandbox sets it and the live village does not, so there a long illness ends with the resident leaving to be nursed by kin. A resident who leaves is taken off the village's records of the living: their open requests are released, their partner is told, and their place in the order of residents is not reused.

**The working day.** `engine/workday.mjs`. Trades keep hours: the baker from four in the morning, the weaver until nine at night. No trade is offered on a Sunday or a feast day, though water is drawn and households carry on. On a feast day the village eats together on the green in the afternoon, from the common stores. The mill and each oven take one worker at a time, and whoever is further through their shift keeps it. A second worker waits, up to two hours. The common buildings wear each day, the village's stock of repair work goes to the worst first, and a mill, oven or well in poor repair turns out a measure less, and nothing once out of use.

**The world beyond.** `engine/visitors.mjs`. A peddler sets out a pack on the green in the open months and trades salt fish for linen. Winter storms strip roofs, lay barley flat and bring down deadwood. Stray cattle eat a ripe field. Only those who see them know, and any of them can drive the cattle out. All of it is decided at dawn by the village's seeded random state.

**The chronicle selects.** `engine/chronicle.mjs` gives every event a salience from 0 to 10 when it happens: a meal 0, an ordinary shift 1, a request or a promise 6 or 7, a wedding or an arrival 8. The village snapshot serves the latest 100 entries at 3 or above. Meals, shifts and other routine stay in residents' memories and the full record, and the Chronicle page fetches them when asked for a kind. The same people doing the same thing within three days is routine the second time, whatever the numbers in the sentence, and talk of one shortage anywhere in the village counts once. A walk to speak to someone that misses them is rated 2 and stays out of the chronicle, unless a request for help depended on it. Missed visits stored before that rule were lowered once at startup. Entries recorded before salience existed were rated once at startup by the same rules in outline. `tools/year.mjs` runs a year under local rules and reports the stores by month and the commonest sentences.

### 13. Memory, warmth and trust

Two parts of Stage 2 came with Stage 3, because courtship needed them.

A memory carries an id, an importance from 1 to 10, its source, the place and the people it concerns. A resident keeps sixty, and when there is no room the least memorable old one goes, so a promise broken outlasts a walk on the green. `recall` in `engine/memory.mjs` scores memories by recency, importance and relevance to the people and place at hand.

Warmth and trust are kept apart in `engine/bonds.mjs`. `relationships` holds warmth, as it always did. It rises with company, rises less between people already close, and fades between residents who have not met for five days. `trust` grows a little with acquaintance, to 58 at most, and otherwise moves on requests as each resident learns of them: a promise made or kept raises it, and a refusal, an unanswered request, or a deadline passed with no word that the work was done lowers it. A resident who hears late that a promise was kept recovers the trust they had given up. Each move is kept in `evidence`, about the person concerned, for a goal about that person to be weighed on. The resident panel shows both numbers for the closest ties.

### 14. Goals persist, and change for reasons

Stage 2 gave each resident goals that last across activities and nights and change only for a reason. It was built on 22 November 2026.

**Two kinds of goal.** `engine/goals.mjs` keeps each resident's goals in force in `goalBook`, each a version with an id. A goal with a condition names one from a fixed vocabulary: the common store holding at least so much of something, being at a place, finishing so many shifts of a kind of work, each within a number of hours up to four days, or one of the resident's open requests being kept. The engine checks these each minute and marks a goal met, missed or overtaken, with what settled it, which the resident remembers. A goal on evidence has no condition. It may name the resident it concerns, and the engine never declares it done. It keeps the evidence that bears on it: the moves in warmth and trust between the two, with what caused each. The founding goals became goals on evidence. `goals` and `desires` still list the goals in force, the calling first, so everything that read them before still does. A changed goal ends the standing instructions chosen under the old one.

**Reviews.** A review is called when one of the resident's goals is settled, when they learn something of importance 8 or more, or at seven in the morning when three days have passed without one. `goal-reviews.mjs` takes the resident who has waited longest, under the clock's lock, records the request, sends it without the lock, and applies the reply under the lock again. The request gives who the resident is, their goals, how settled goals ended, ten recalled memories and the evidence about anyone a goal concerns, each with a key, and the vocabulary of conditions. The model replies with a decision for each goal (keep, revise or retire) and at most two new ones, as structured output. The engine checks every decision: a condition must fit the vocabulary and the village, at most four goals may be in force, and a goal about another resident is changed only when the review cites keys it was given. A failed check sets that decision aside and the rest apply. A revised goal keeps its id and takes the next version. Reviews have their own cap, 30,000 tokens a rolling hour by default (`GOAL_REVIEW_TOKENS_PER_HOUR`), separate from action choices. Without `CLAUDE_KEY`, after a failure, or between reviews, residents keep the goals they have. `DALECREST_GOAL_MODEL` can name another Claude model that supports structured output.

**A resident's own words.** A review may return one sentence in the resident's voice about its most important change. It is published only if it is one plain sentence with no link or markup, as an "In their words" chronicle entry that says it is what the resident said. It is their stated intention, not a verified motive.

**The chain in PostgreSQL.** Every memory a resident forms is stored in `dalecrest.observations`, linked to the chronicle entry it records. `dalecrest.goal_reviews` keeps each review exactly as sent and received, with its cost and result. `dalecrest.goal_versions` keeps every version of every goal with the memories and evidence it rests on and the review that made it. Each action trace records the goal versions and memories its request was given. The view `dalecrest.goal_chain` joins them, so one query follows a memory to the goal it supported, the choice that goal informed, the outcome and who witnessed it. [Follow a goal](decision-tracing.md#follow-a-goal)

**What the action model sees.** Its request now carries the goals in force, with any condition, and six recalled memories with their dates in place of the five latest, along with the resident's own life and household.

### 15. The common store has a building, a keeper and an allowance

Dalecrest holds its goods in common: nobody is paid, and nothing is bought or sold between residents. Stage 3a gave the common store a place. It was built on 22 November 2026, after the live village was found taking twice the bread it needed: the engine offered a loaf at any hunger, and the Decisions API often took one. The four decisions it rested on are recorded as open decisions 12 to 15 in the [roadmap](roadmap.md).

**Meals wait for hunger.** No meal is offered to a resident whose hunger is under 30 (`MEAL_FLOOR` in `engine/storehouse.mjs`). A loaf takes 53 off, so a meal before that is waste.

**The storehouse.** Every village has one, on the best free lot between the green and the oven, chosen by rule so a stored village and a new one with the same map get the same building. It holds the finished goods residents take for themselves: loaves, linen, salt fish and smoked game, and leather. Barley stays in the barn and granaries, flour and firewood at the oven, water at the well, and fresh and cooked food at the hall. It wears and is mended like the other common buildings.

**Goods are carried in.** A shift that makes storehouse goods ends with the worker carrying them there, as the last leg of the same activity, so it needs no decision of its own and a decision trace follows it to the end. The goods count when they are on the shelf, and the shift is recorded then, so the stock change in its record is the one the shelves show. Someone interrupted on the way sets the goods down at once. The record keeps the place the work was done.

**The allowance.** At dawn each household is allowed 1.25 loaves for each grown resident and one for each child old enough to eat (`ALLOWANCE`). An allowance not drawn keeps for one more day, so nobody can lay up a store of their own. A hungry resident draws what is due, as far as the larder has room, and eats one loaf: "Drawing the household's bread at the storehouse" is a meal, so a standing instruction can cover it. A household with children can also lay in its bread without eating. A lodger draws one loaf at a time and eats it there. The village opened its allowance once, with a day's worth for everyone, in `world.allowanceSince`; after that every allowance comes at dawn, so stepping the clock in large or small steps agrees.

**A keeper.** As soon as a village has a storehouse and no keeper, a storekeeper comes up the road, whatever the season, and sleeps at the storehouse until there is a cottage to spare. The keeper counts the shelves about twice a day and lends a hand elsewhere between. Travellers looking for work never take up the trade. The keeper is not a gate: anyone may draw their allowance when the keeper is away, and the engine holds them to it.

**Knowing what is short.** Anyone at the storehouse sees what is on the shelves. The count is a fact like the oven count, with its witness and minute, and can be passed on in conversation. A count of fewer than six loaves lets whoever holds it ask the baker, and only the baker, to bake five loaves for the storehouse. The request is kept when the loaves reach the shelf. The action model is given the household's bread still due and the latest count the resident has seen or heard.

**What changed under local rules.** Over 240 days on seed 26111 residents ate about as many meals as before, 1.54 a day against 1.55, and every loaf drawn was eaten or was still in a larder. With larders kept stocked they ate more bread and fewer cooked meals at the hall, so the barley ran lower: at the end of March in a year's run the stores held 8 days of food, against 27 before. Local rules stand in for the Decisions API here. In the live village the allowance caps what was a waste.
